How Your ADU Connects to Sewer, Water & Electric in Tempe (and What It Costs)

By Kris OntiverosSeptember 27, 20267 min read

Tempe's ADU rules read a lot like Phoenix's on the pipes and wires, with two things layered on top: every casita must have its own address, and the City charges a water development fee whenever a project creates an additional dwelling unit, meter or no meter. Here is how each connection works on a Tempe lot, what we verify, and what it costs.

The short version

In Tempe, your ADU shares the sewer and water service your home already has, gets its own SRP or APS electric meter as our standard, and gets its own address because the City requires one for every ADU.

Sewer
Tie into your home's existing sewer line. A separate tap to the main is a case-by-case City determination, not the default.
Water
Tie into your home's water line, after the meter. One meter per lot unless the Community Development Director approves a second.
Electric
New, separate SRP or APS meter for the ADU. The City defers to the utility on metering and requires a unique address for the unit either way.

Tempe lots are older and tighter than most of the East Valley, which usually means shorter trench runs but more mature landscaping, hardscape, and older sewer pipe in the way. Distance is still the biggest driver of cost; existing conditions are a close second here.

Sewer: share the lateral

Tempe's zoning code requires residential sewer, water, and utility connections to the ADU "in conformance with City standards" (Tempe Zoning and Development Code 3-402). Your ADU connects to your home's existing sewer line on private property. The City's sewer code lets the City Manager decide whether a separate building needs its own tap to the main, but on a normal residential lot the shared lateral is the expected path, and we confirm it with the Permit Center at plan review. Sewer is billed on your water account, so there's no separate sewer account for the unit.

What we verify at the site visit:

  • Grade. Sewer flows by gravity. If the ADU sits lower than your home's line, a small ejector pump may be needed.
  • Pipe size and condition. We confirm a 4-inch line. Many Tempe homes date to the 1960s and 70s, so a camera inspection is common; damaged clay pipe is replaced as a change order.
  • Easements. State law bars building an ADU over a public utility easement without written consent from the utilities that use it. We check the plat before we place the unit.
  • Septic lots. Rare in Tempe, but if your home is on septic, Maricopa County Environmental Services reviews the system before the City will issue a permit.

Water: one meter, and a development fee to plan for

Your ADU tees off your home's water line after the existing City meter, with its own shutoff valve. Tempe's water code says multiple meters serving a single service aren't permitted unless the Community Development Director approves, so one meter per lot is the default. The City has told Council its existing infrastructure can handle ADU demand, and meter sizing is settled at plan review under the plumbing code.

  • Water development fee. Tempe's fee code applies the water development fee to work on an existing home that "creates one or more additional dwelling units," which an ADU does, even without a new meter. It's charged by meter size and currently runs from about $1,650 at 5/8-inch to about $2,470 at 3/4-inch (Tempe fee schedule, Appendix A). The sewer development fee is currently set at zero. We confirm the amount with the Permit Center and pass it through at cost.
  • Meter size check. If the combined fixture count exceeds your current meter, an upsize is required, and the City's tap and meter fees apply to the larger size.
  • Optional private submeter. Renting the unit? A submeter on the ADU's line lets you track its water use and bill a tenant. The City doesn't read it; you do. Arizona's tenant-billing rules are in our separate meter vs. submeter guide.

Electric: its own meter, and its own address regardless

Tempe's ADU FAQ answers the separate-meter question with "refer to your utility provider's requirements," so the City neither requires nor blocks a second meter. SRP serves most of Tempe and APS serves a portion; either will set a second residential meter once the City has permitted the unit. A separate meter is our standard because it gives the casita its own bill and adds no load to your home's panel.

  • SOKO applies to SRP or APS, and the utility sets the connection point. We trench and install conduit to their design; they pull wire and set the meter.
  • Unique address, required. Tempe's zoning code requires a unique address for each ADU, posted near its entrance, whether or not it has its own meter. Building Safety assigns it during permitting, and SOKO handles the request.
  • Plan on added utility lead time. Scheduling is set by SRP or APS, not SOKO.

Option: power from your home's panel

We can instead feed the ADU from your home's electrical panel, with one shared bill, when a load calculation shows your panel has room, usually a 200-amp service. It's often a fit for family or guest use, and if you have rooftop solar it's usually the better economics. Many older Tempe homes are on 100-amp or 125-amp service, in which case a new ADU meter is usually better value than upgrading the house.

Example: a 40-foot run with shared sewer and water trench, a separate 40-foot electric trench, a new meter and the required address comes to about $13,300 before City and utility fees.

What moves the number

  • Existing conditions. Old clay sewer pipe, a shallow lateral, and mature hardscape are the most common Tempe surprises, which is why we camera older lines before pricing.
  • Distance. Every foot between your home's lines and the ADU is priced. We place the ADU and route trenches to keep runs short.
  • Your water meter. An adequate existing meter means no upsize; the development fee applies either way.
  • Utility requirements. SRP or APS sets the meter location and service route, which can lengthen the electric trench.

What this pricing assumes

  • Pricing applies to City of Tempe projects. For other cities see our posts for Phoenix, Scottsdale, Mesa, Chandler, Gilbert, and unincorporated Maricopa County.
  • Run lengths are measured at the site visit and confirmed on the permit site plan.
  • Utility and City fees are billed at cost, as invoiced.
  • Excludes gas service, relocation of existing utilities, landscape and hardscape restoration beyond the trench, and repairs to existing home systems.
  • Requirements reflect Tempe Ordinance O2024.51 (Zoning and Development Code 3-402), the City fee schedule, and utility policies as of September 2026 and may change.
